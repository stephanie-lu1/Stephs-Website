Fall 2025 - Investigating Model Performance across Categories

Motivation: "Early experience with low-pass filtered images facilitates visual category learning in a neural network model" (Jinsi et al 2023).

(A): Global shape exemplars
(B): Blur manipulation across training
(C): Blurring across grayscale condition
(D): Blurring across color condition

When humans are born, we have poor spatial acuity, color, and contrast sensitivity. As we develop, these visual traits improve. To account for this in model training, a blurring and grayscale condition were implemented onto images in the Ecoset dataset. Example images from the basic level categories “dog”, “fish”, “car”, “truck”, blurred at three example values for sigma. Note that larger sigma values give rise to blurrier images, while σ = 0 results in an intact, non-blurred image. Examples of the same images are shown for the (C) grayscale and (D) color conditions (Jinsi et al 2023)

The results from training show that for the grayscale images, there is a benefit to having blurred images compared to the non-blurred. However, for the color images, all models converge to the same level of performance, regardless of blur.

Clearly, we see that the effect of blur is dependent on color as well as other model training parameters. When color is present, learning may be based on that feature, instead of considering shape and texture. In past literature, it was found that CNNs are biased towards texture and less about shape, but humans have shown the converse (Gierhos et al 2023). When it is in grayscale condition, texture and shape are considered more.

We should also consider some limitations for comparing human vision to neural network simulations. When the CNN was trained, there are labels that provide supervision in training; it learns the ground truth during training. However, it was found in Elimas & Quinn (1994) that infants can distinguish between visually-similar categories. Addtionally, in the dataset, there is only a limited number of viewing conditions that are presented even in the 5.5+ million images. Arguably, humans have had much much more training data across their life, so there is a lack of generalization able to made. All this is to say that we cannot perfectly compare humans and CNNs.

My Work:





There are six models from Jinsi et al. (2023): three trained on grayscale images (without blur, with linear blur, and with nonlinear blur) and three trained on color images (without blur, with linear blur, and with nonlinear blur). I did not re-train them, I only took the pre-trained models and evaluated their performance over ten trials each. It is important to note that I also only took the maximum performance at a given time from each one of these trials from the validation training set. That set me up to get one value per basic-level category for each model condition. From there, I computed the mean accuracy from each model condition for each category. The models trained on the color conditions did perform the best, like the paper.

Additionally, note that there is a discrepancy between the paper and my results. This is where the limitation of using the maximum performance time point comes in. I only got one value where we can see in the paper, the accuracy is measured across all time points per epoch (thus getting a curve). It is not a surprise that my accuracy values were higher.

From there, I separated the 565 basic-level categories into 14 superordinate categories but then modified it to 15. (Figure A shows the initial 14 catgories, but I added on another one when performing t-statistic calculation later). Some superordinate categories had more labels than others due to the nature of the dataset. I computed the same average accuracy across these categories to get the general idea on which categories the models were doing the best on.

I was then curious about the effects between the blurred condition vs. the colored condition; that is, is there any interaction between the conditions? So, I computed the t-statistics for each superordinate category. There is a significant t-statistic for the majority of the categories in the colored condition, but only when there was a blur applied; this aligned with the paper as expected.

A few things to note include that my organization of superordinate categories was completely subjective. One category I was having issues with was the plants/foods categories - some plants can be classified as food, so which category would I put that in? I ended up breaking it down even further and adding a "plants as foods" category, which were the plants that could act as food items as well. I am currently in the process of continuing to break down the superordinate categories even more - some things I have done are breaking "objects" (miscalleanous) into natural and artifical objects, dividing animals and tools by size, and sorting tools/utilities by complexity. Future work can continue this breakdown, as well as having a second coder of the labels into categories.

Spring 2026 - Coming soon!

Motivation: "VGGFace2: A dataset for recognising faces across pose and age" (Cao et al 2018)

One aspect of my interest of human social cognition + AI includes the question of how well do models perform at the task of face recognition? To answer this question, I will be fine-tuning the previous ResNet-50 model trained on the Ecoset dataset onto the VGGface2 dataset.


The VGGface2 dataset consists of over 3 million images among over 9 thousand participants. Each partipant has anywhere from 80 - 843 images, accounting for variation in pose, age, illumination, and ethnicity. It is worth mentioning that the subjects in the dataset were only the ones who had a sufficient number of imaegs of them available on Google Image Search; that is to say many of them are celebrities/well known public figures.

In the paper, several ResNet-50 models trained on previous face datasets ( examples include but are not limited to VGGFace and MS-Celeb-1M) were trained to provide a similarity score across a feature template of subjects varying in pose and age. It was found that the model trained on the VGGFace2 dataset provided the best result with the highest similarity score.
